How it works

For every dot in the picture, the app works out how much light your projector really puts out, using your readings. It multiplies that light by the screen's gain, adds the ambient light, and shows both screens on your monitor using the same brightness scale. Think of it as photographing both screens with the same camera settings: it tells you truthfully which is brighter, by how much, and how room light affects each one, but it won't look the same as sitting in your room.

What it calculates exactly

  • Higher gain brightens everything. Going from 1.2 to 1.5 gain makes white, black and every shade in between 25% brighter.
  • In a dark room, contrast stays the same, because black gets brighter by the same amount as white.
  • A bigger screen spreads the same light over more area. Going from 120 to 150 inches makes the picture (120 ÷ 150)² = 0.64 times as bright, unless higher gain makes up for it.
  • Ambient light adds the same amount of light everywhere, so it washes out dark scenes first. That's why blocking ambient light can matter more than gain in a bright room.
  • Both pictures use the same brightness scale, so the difference you see between A and B is real.
  • Your own measured curve is used exactly when you load a ColourSpace or CSV file, including shadows that are darker or brighter than the standard.
  • HDR uses the published standards: PQ (ST 2084) for HDR brightness and the ITU reference tone-mapping curve (BT.2408) into your projector's real peak, worked out on the full picture so the preview size never changes it.
  • Checked independently. A separate re-implementation of the formulas and tests of the pixels drawn on screen agree with the app. That shows the math is done correctly. How close the picture is to your room still depends on your readings and on the best guesses listed next.

Best guesses

  • Gain numbers from screen makers are often a bit high. For the best result, measure a sample of the new screen (Screen B → Fine-tune). The "How sure" line under the picture tells you which numbers are measured and which are estimates.
  • Sitting to the side uses one number per screen. Real screens vary a little.
  • Bigger screen: if you zoom the lens to fill it, some projectors lose a little more light than the math shows.
  • Blocks ambient light is one number. Real screens block light from the ceiling better than light from behind you.
  • Dynamic iris is shown as a perfect iris that closes as much as each picture allows.
  • HDR tone mapping uses the standard curve. Your projector or video processor (Lumagen, madVR and others) tone-maps in its own way, so bright parts can look a bit different.
  • Built-in HDR pictures other than "HDR brightness steps" are SDR photos given an HDR grade by this app. Use your own HDR frame grabs for real HDR.
  • Not shown: color changes caused by the screen, screen texture or sparkle, and HDR brightness above your monitor's white.

What depends on your monitor

  • Black. OLED monitors and most phones can show true black, so dark scenes look right. Most other monitors show black as dark gray, which makes dark scenes look lighter than on a projector in a dark room. On those, compare A with B instead of judging how black it looks.
  • Real brightness. Auto makes the brighter screen as bright as your monitor can go. To see real brightness, measure your monitor and choose Match my monitor.
  • Wide color. On wide-color monitors (most Macs, OLEDs and newer laptops) the app shows HDR colors beyond sRGB. On standard monitors those colors are clipped.
  • How your eyes adjust. In a dark room your eyes get used to one screen, so the difference can feel smaller than it does here.
  • Closest look. Use an OLED monitor in a dark room, choose Match my monitor, load your own readings, measure a sample of the new screen, and compare with screenshots from movies you watch.
  • Pictures. The people pictures are AI-made photos. For the best test, add screenshots from movies you watch (not HDR screenshots).

How to take readings

  1. Turn the projector on 20–30 minutes early, in the picture mode you watch with.
  2. Sit in your main seat and point your meter at the middle of the screen.
  3. Show an all-white picture and write down the number. That's your White reading.
  4. Show an all-black picture and write down the number. That's your Black reading.
  5. Do this for each setting (Iris 2, Iris 3 and so on) and save each one as a reading. You can save up to 10.
  6. Dynamic iris: measure the all-black picture with it on, and again with it off.
  7. Ambient light: see "How do I measure it?" in the Ambient light panel.

    How is HDR shown?

    Each pixel's HDR brightness (PQ, in nits) is fitted into your projector's real peak with the ITU reference tone-mapping curve (BT.2408, first published in BT.2390), keeping each color's hue. The "HDR brightness steps" picture and your own HDR frame grabs (16-bit PNG) are real HDR. The other built-in pictures are SDR photos given an HDR grade by this app: white at 203 nits and highlights up to 1,000 nits.

    What do these mean?
    Gain
    How much light the screen bounces back to you. A 1.5 screen is 25% brighter than a 1.2 screen (1.5 ÷ 1.2 = 1.25).
    Size
    The screen's diagonal in inches. A bigger screen spreads the same light over more area, so it looks dimmer.
    Screen type
    Fills in typical numbers for that kind of screen. Open Fine-tune to type in your screen's exact numbers.
    Blocks ambient light
    How much lamp and window light the screen keeps off the picture. A plain white screen is 0%. Only matters when ambient light is on.
    Half as bright at
    How far to the side you can sit before the picture looks half as bright. 0 means it looks the same from every seat. Only matters when you sit to the side.
